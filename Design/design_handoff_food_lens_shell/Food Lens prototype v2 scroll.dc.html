Try it

Scroll the phone slowly

Watch the dark strip under the viewfinder. At the moment less than half the camera is on screen it flips from Reading the camera to the food's name plus Camera paused, the pulse stops, and the "Frames sent" counter freezes while "Skipped" starts climbing.

One strip, two jobs

The sticky strip under the viewfinder reports the loop while the camera is up, then becomes the camera's stand-in once it scrolls away — food name, score, and a button back. Same 44px row either way, so nothing jumps, and the food's name is never printed twice on one screen.

The answer is above the fold again

First pass put a 440px viewfinder on top, which pushed the verdict to roughly 500px down an 844px screen — you landed on the camera and had to scroll to find out anything. That quietly undid the whole point of "say the answer once, immediately". The camera is now 336px, so band, sentence and number all land in the first screen with the chart's top edge peeking to invite the scroll.

The alternative was a verdict overlaid on the viewfinder. Rejected: it reintroduces the same number in two places — the exact fault the review doc opens with — and sets a 21px sentence and a 50px figure over live food photography.

The voice bar is pinned, not dragged

It sticks to the bottom of the viewport at every scroll position, so the mic is always in the thumb arc — without a gesture to learn. Tap the transcript line to expand the log; it grows upward and the page keeps its place.

Why this is better than my sheet

  • No gesture to teach. Peek / half / full was a model the user had to discover. Scrolling is a model they already have.
  • It deletes a whole class of bug. The draggable version's handle could out-grow its own sheet and annihilate the content pane. A sticky bar cannot do that.
  • Visibility becomes the gate. A draggable sheet over a fixed camera has no honest signal for "the user isn't looking at the camera." Scroll position is exactly that signal, for free.

What it costs to build

  • A fourth disarm reason. F5 exposes disarmReason: "idle" | "provider" | null. This adds "offscreen" — and unlike the other two it must re-arm automatically when the camera returns, never via rearm() and never showing a "Scan again" chip. Getting that wrong makes scrolling back feel broken.
  • It pairs with the 60-second stash, not against it. Scroll away and back inside a minute and stashRef should re-show the last match rather than re-identifying — so a scroll costs zero calls, not one.
  • Hysteresis, or it thrashes. A single 50% threshold flips repeatedly during a slow scroll. Pause at 50% leaving, resume at 60% entering, and hold the resume for one interval so a flick past the camera doesn't fire a request.
  • It does not replace the idle disarm. A camera parked on screen while nobody looks still burns frames; AUTO_DISARM_MS stays.
  • The counters are a demo, not a metric. Real numbers need instrumenting the loop. Worth doing — this is the cheapest spend story in the product.
  • Never put the scroll ratio in component state. My first build did, and it froze the tab: a render per scroll event, where the render itself swapped the pill text and mounted the pulse — scroll → render → layout → scroll, until the main thread died. The ratio now lives in a ref, the readout is written imperatively, measurement is coalesced to one animation frame, and only the on/off boolean re-renders. Any real implementation will hit this.
  • And keep expensive paint out of the scroller. Same crash had two accomplices: a backdrop-filter blur on the sticky header (re-rasterising the blurred strip every frame of every scroll) and a drop-shadow filter on a 132px glyph. Both were decorative; both are gone. A viewfinder that scrolls is a compositing problem as much as a layout one.

One thing I'd push on

Gating on visibility is also a privacy feature, not just a cost one, and it is more legible than anything in the current disclosure copy: "frames go out only while the camera is on your screen" is a sentence a patient can verify with their own thumb. That deserves to be the headline of the disclosure sheet rather than a line in a card — and it is worth asking whether it changes what the AI-data disclosure has to claim.
